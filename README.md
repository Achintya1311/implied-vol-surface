Educational research on historical data. Not investment advice. Not a SEBI-registered research analyst.

**Question:** on real (not synthetic) NSE option chains, how often do raw quotes violate a no-arbitrage bound before any filtering, and does the fitted surface show real butterfly/calendar violations?
**Method:** solve implied vol and Greeks per contract, then check the fitted smile/surface for static-arbitrage (butterfly, calendar) violations, run against six real home-connection captures (RELIANCE, NIFTY) via `--provider nse_raw`.

The fitted surface shows 26 (RELIANCE) and 149 (NIFTY) real butterfly violations, zero calendar violations.

## What this is

Solves implied volatility per contract, computes Greeks analytically and cross-checks them against finite differences, then assembles smile, term structure and surface.

Illiquid strikes produce garbage IVs that look like real structure, so filtering and butterfly/calendar arbitrage checks run before anything is plotted.

## Correctness gate

IV round-trips (price to IV to price), analytic Greeks match finite differences, no static-arbitrage violations survive filtering.

This is the test that decides whether the repo is finished. A result that has not passed it is a draft.

## Findings

Day 1 (chain fetcher): both live sources were tried end to end from this sandbox, not just written and assumed working.

- **NSE's endpoint reaches, but is empty.** A `requests.Session` GET to `nseindia.com` to warm a cookie returns a real 170KB homepage (HTTP 200) - it is not a network-level block. The follow-up GET to `/api/option-chain-equities?symbol=RELIANCE` also returns HTTP 200, but the body is `{}`. No error, no rate-limit message, just no data - NSE's edge is discriminating on something beyond headers and a warmed cookie (likely TLS/JA3 fingerprinting or a JS challenge this session can't run). `NSEChainProvider` surfaces that as `ProviderError: unexpected NSE payload ... no 'records' key`, which is at least legible, but it is not a fix.
- **yfinance genuinely lists no options for NSE single names.** `yfinance.Ticker("RELIANCE.NS").options` comes back empty, live, from this sandbox. This isn't a sandbox artifact - Yahoo's options data for NSE-listed equities is documented elsewhere as unreliable/absent; RELIANCE.NS reproduces that directly. The cross-check source in the README's original data-sources list works in code (see `TestYFinanceChainProviderOffline`) but has nothing real to check against for this project's target names.
- **Net effect:** the fixture path is not a fallback for this project, it is the only path that currently produces data. `fixtures/chains/RELIANCE.json` is a hand-assembled (Black-Scholes-priced, realistic equity skew) payload run through the real `parse_nse_payload()`, not a live capture - see Limitations.

Day 2 (IV solver, `volsurface/iv.py` + `volsurface/solve.py`): Newton-Raphson with analytic vega, falling back to Brent's method (`scipy.optimize.brentq`, bounded to sigma in `[1e-6, 5.0]`) when a Newton step's own size (not the price residual - see below) hasn't shrunk below tolerance. Solved against the bid/ask mid, not `last_price`, since a stale last trade is a worse target than a live two-sided quote.

- **A price-residual convergence check is the wrong test for deep ITM/OTM.** The first version of this solver declared Newton "converged" once the price residual fell under an absolute tolerance. That is wrong wherever vega is small: a deep ITM call's price is nearly flat in sigma, so many different sigma values reproduce the same price to 1e-8 - the loop stopped early at whichever sigma it happened to be visiting, not at the true one. Caught by `TestSolveIvRoundTrip::test_price_to_iv_to_price_round_trips[0.15-CE-1000.0]` failing with a recovered IV of 0.197 against a true 0.15 - a confidently wrong number, not a crash. Fixed by judging convergence on Newton's own step size in sigma, which is only small when the *sigma* estimate has actually stopped moving.
- **Some deep ITM/OTM prices have no recoverable IV in double precision at all**, not just a hard-to-find one. `S*N(d1)` and `K*e^{-rT}*N(d2)` are both O(1000) while their difference (the option's extrinsic value) can be O(1e-10) or smaller; below a certain moneyness/vol/tenor combination, every candidate sigma from near-zero to the search's upper bound prices to the *same* floating-point floor value - there's no sign change for Brent to bracket, because there's no distinguishable root to find. `solve_iv` checks for this explicitly (price within `1e-6` of its own no-arbitrage floor) and raises `NoSolution` naming it, rather than either crashing on Brent's bracket-not-found error or quietly returning whatever sigma Newton happened to stop at. See `TestDeepMoneynessNumericalFloor`.
- **Two contracts in the committed `RELIANCE.json` fixture have no solution for a more mundane reason: cent-rounding.** The nearest-expiry `1250 CE` and `1550 PE` are so deep ITM (5 days to expiry) that their bid/ask mid, rounded to two decimals when the fixture was hand-assembled, lands about half a paisa *below* the theoretical no-arbitrage floor implied by `r=6.5%`. This isn't a numerical-floor case (the two are 0.0025 and 0.0005 apart, well above the 1e-6 double-precision threshold above) - it's what real tick-rounded quotes do to a theoretical bound near the money's edge. `solve_iv` still refuses rather than returning a number for a price that, by its own arithmetic, implies riskless arbitrage. `python -m volsurface.solve --underlying RELIANCE` reports both by name; 50/52 contracts solve.
- **The recovered smile is internally consistent, and shows the skew the fixture was built with.** Put and call IVs at the same strike/expiry agree to 3-4 decimal places away from the tails (put-call parity holds, as it must for a solver built from the same closed-form pricer on both sides), and IV rises as strike falls - the "downward equity skew" the fixture's docstring claims. That agreement is a property of solving against a self-consistent synthetic chain, not evidence the solver would behave this well against noisy real quotes - see Limitations.

Day 3 (Greeks, `volsurface/greeks.py`): delta, gamma and theta added analytically (vega was already `iv.bs_vega`, reused rather than redefined); every one of the four is cross-checked against a central finite difference of `iv.bs_price` on the same parameters.

- **A relative tolerance is the wrong test for the cross-check, for the same reason it was wrong for IV convergence in Day 2.** Delta, gamma and vega all go genuinely near zero deep ITM/OTM - the first attempt at this cross-check used `pytest.approx(..., rel=1e-6)` and failed at strikes 150+ points away from spot, not because the finite difference was inaccurate (the absolute gap was ~1e-5) but because dividing a tiny absolute gap by a tiny Greek value produced a large relative one. `tests/test_greeks.py` uses absolute tolerances throughout instead, sized about 10x above the largest absolute gap actually observed across strikes 1250-1550, sigma 0.15-0.6, and both 30-day and 5-day tenors.
- **The finite-difference bump for a second derivative (gamma) has to be larger than for a first derivative (delta, vega), not the same.** A first pass used one bump size for everything; gamma's central-difference numerator is a price value minus itself twice, and at the Day-2 bump size that subtraction was cancelling away real digits, not just noise past them - caught by comparing the same bump's delta accuracy (fine) against its gamma accuracy (visibly worse) on the same inputs. `FD_BUMP_S_REL` is used directly for delta/vega and ten times over for gamma; see `volsurface/greeks.py` for the reasoning.
- **`compute_chain_greeks` initially produced NaN Greeks for every row, including the 50 that solved.** The row-skip check compared the IV table's `error` column to `None`, but a `None` written into a DataFrame column round-trips as float `NaN` once read back through `iterrows()` (confirmed by inspecting the column's dtype directly) - `is not None` is always true for a NaN, so every row looked unsolved. Fixed with `pd.notna()`, which treats both shapes as "no error" correctly; caught immediately by `test_full_fixture_chain_cross_checks_within_tolerance` asserting no NaNs on the solved rows, not by inspection.
- **`python -m volsurface.solve --underlying RELIANCE --greeks` cross-checks all 50 solved contracts; the worst analytic-vs-finite-difference gap on this fixture is ~8e-6**, comfortably inside the test suite's absolute tolerances. The two contracts Day 2 couldn't solve still get no Greeks either - there's no sigma to differentiate the pricer at without one.
- **Theta still assumes `q=0` throughout**, same as delta, gamma and vega inherit from `iv.py`'s `DEFAULT_DIVIDEND_YIELD` - a real dividend-paying name would need every Greek here recomputed with the actual yield, not just the price.

Day 4 (surface construction, `volsurface/surface.py`): a liquidity filter (open interest and bid/ask relative spread, both documented thresholds) keeps one out-of-the-money IV per (expiry, strike), a monotone cubic (PCHIP) fits a smile across the surviving strikes for each expiry, and the fitted surface is checked for butterfly (per expiry) and calendar (across expiries) static arbitrage.

- **The liquidity filter matters even though open interest alone doesn't explain the noise it removes.** The near-term (5-day) expiry's deep-wing contracts (`1250`, `1500`, `1525`, `1550`) produce a visibly jagged IV smile (0.226 -> 0.232 -> 0.279 -> 0.327 moving out from the money) - not because open interest is low there (it is, 500-5975 vs 14071-44997 at the liquid core), but because their quoted premium is tiny (5-11 paise) and NSE's real tick size (0.05) is a large fraction of that price. The far expiry's wings have the *same* open interest (500 at both 1250 and 1550) but a *clean* monotone skew, because 30 extra days of time value makes the premium large enough that the same tick size is noise, not signal. A filter on open interest alone would keep the near-term garbage (14071+ OI strikes are clean either way) and would drop the far expiry's fine wings for no reason; the relative-spread threshold (`<= 20%`) is what actually separates real skew from tick-rounding artifacts here - open interest is a weaker, complementary signal, not sufficient by itself. After filtering: 5/26 near-term rows and 11/26 far-term rows survive per `python -m volsurface.solve --underlying RELIANCE --surface`.
- **The butterfly check on the fitted smile is honest, but less sensitive than it looks - for the same reason Day 2/3 already found.** `check_butterfly` re-prices the fitted IV smile back into call prices and checks convexity in strike. Re-deriving a price from IV washes out exactly the tick-rounding noise the liquidity filter exists to catch: those same jagged near-term wing strikes have near-zero vega (a large sigma error round-trips to a tiny price error, per Day 2/3's own finding), so a butterfly built from IV-derived prices can look clean even where the underlying quotes were genuinely noisy. A supplementary check converting the *raw* quoted mid prices to a common call curve via put-call parity (never through the solved IV at all) was run by hand against this fixture and also found no violation - but that is a property of this fixture (it was priced from one self-consistent Black-Scholes surface before tick-rounding, so the rounding never got large enough to break convexity even in raw price space), not evidence the check would catch a real arbitrage. Neither check had, as of Day 4, been run against a chain with a genuine violation to confirm it fires - only the synthetic cases in `tests/test_surface.py` (a deliberately spiked IV, a deliberately inverted term structure) did that. Day 8's `volsurface/audit.py` closes this specific gap by planting the same kind of violation into this fixture's own real strikes rather than a from-scratch smile - see Day 8 Findings for what that does and doesn't prove.
- **Calendar arbitrage was checked at every strike shared by both expiries' fitted domains and found none**, which is expected here: the far expiry's total variance (`iv**2 * T`) is roughly 5-6x the near expiry's at the same strikes, driven mostly by the ~6.4x longer tenor, comfortably clearing the non-decreasing requirement. This was not yet a meaningful stress test of the check as of Day 4 - a real chain with a genuine term-structure inversion (e.g. around an earnings date, which Day 7 looked at directly for skew) had never been run through it. Day 8's `audit_calendar_check_fires_on_real_fixture` closes this the same way as the butterfly gap above: shrinking this fixture's own real far-expiry IVs rather than building a synthetic pair from scratch.
- **"No arbitrage violations survive filtering" is true for this fixture, not proven in general.** It was built from a single consistent Black-Scholes surface (see Day 1 Findings), so there was never a real static-arbitrage violation for the filter to remove - the filter's value demonstrated here is making the IV *smile* interpretable and interpolatable, not clearing a violation that was actually present.

Day 5 (visualization, `volsurface/plot.py`): a per-expiry smile PNG, an ATM term-structure PNG, a static 3D surface PNG, and a standalone interactive 3D surface HTML (Plotly bundled inline, opens with no network), all built directly from Day 4's already-fitted, already-arbitrage-checked `SurfaceResult` - nothing here refits anything.

- **The ATM term structure on this fixture is inverted (downward-sloping), and that is consistent with, not contradicted by, "no calendar arbitrage found."** Near-expiry (T=0.014y) ATM IV is 0.235; far-expiry (T=0.096y) ATM IV is 0.215 - IV itself falls as tenor lengthens. Day 4's calendar check passes anyway because it compares *total variance* (`iv**2 * T`), not IV directly: the far expiry's ~6.4x longer tenor more than offsets its lower IV, so total variance still rises from near to far. A plot of raw IV against tenor would make this fixture look arbitrage-violating at a glance; it isn't, and the term-structure PNG's axis is labelled ATM IV, not total variance, specifically so a reader isn't misled by that gap - see Limitations for what a mismatch between the two would look like on a real chain.
- **The "3D surface" is a set of disconnected per-expiry lines, not a jointly-fitted sheet, and the plot says so rather than implying otherwise.** Day 4 never interpolates *across* expiries - only *within* one via PCHIP over strike - so there is no fitted value for, say, (K=1400, T=0.05) sitting between the two expiries this fixture has. Filling that gap would require a second interpolation dimension (in tenor) that no prior day built or arbitrage-checked; adding it silently to make the picture look more like a "surface" would show a shape the pipeline never actually verified. The plotted view is therefore honestly sparse: two lines in (strike, tenor, IV) space, each traceable back to Day 4's own fitted smile.
- **With only two expiries in the committed fixture, every view here is thin by construction.** The 3D plot in particular would look far more like a genuine surface with 4-6 expiries; two lines is enough to prove the plumbing (data in, PNG/HTML out, honest about what isn't interpolated) but not enough to show what a real multi-expiry NSE chain's surface looks like. Untested with more than two expiries.
- **Both PNG and HTML are produced from the same underlying per-expiry data, but only the 3D view gets an HTML export.** Reading an exact (strike, tenor, IV) value off a static 3D plot is close to impossible past 2 expiries because of projection and occlusion - the reason the 2D slices (smile, term structure) don't get their own HTML is that a static line plot already shows every value that a plot of that shape can show.

Day 6 (daily history job, `volsurface/history.py` + `.github/workflows/daily-snapshot.yml`): a small CLI runs the existing pipeline once (chain -> solve -> surface, reusing `volsurface.plot.term_structure_points` for ATM IV so this row and the Day 5 chart can never disagree about what "ATM" means) and appends one JSON row per underlying to `fixtures/history/<UNDERLYING>.json`. A GitHub Actions workflow runs it on a weekday cron, attempting a live NSE capture first (best effort, `continue-on-error`) and always falling back to the committed fixture, then commits the updated history file if it changed.

- **Each row carries two dates, not one, and that split is the whole design.** `run_date` (when the job executed) is the key rows are upserted on - re-running the job twice in a day updates that day's row instead of duplicating it. `data_date` (the chain snapshot's own valuation date, from `ChainSnapshot.timestamp`) is *not* part of that key. Keying on `data_date` instead would have been wrong: the only provider that works from this sandbox (`fixture`) always carries the same fixed `data_date`, so every day's row would collapse into one and the "daily" job would never actually accumulate anything.
- **That design choice is also this day's honest finding: right now, every row is numerically identical.** Ran the CLI for three simulated days (`--as-of 2026-09-25/26/27`) against the committed fixture - `run_date` advanced each time and the history file grew to three rows, exactly as designed, but `atm_iv_30d`, `atm_iv_near`, `term_slope` and `spot` are bit-for-bit the same in all three, because `data_date` never moved (see Day 1's Findings for why live capture is blocked here). The job is proven to accumulate; the *content* it accumulates won't be informative until a live capture succeeds at least once, on this sandbox or on GitHub's own runners - untested which, since this sandbox's own network restrictions don't necessarily hold there. Left in the repo as a real, working three-row history, then reset to the single genuine row this CLI actually produced today before committing, so the fixture isn't presented as three real trading days it isn't.
- **`atm_iv_30d` is "the fitted expiry closest to 30 calendar days", not an interpolation to exactly 30 days.** With only two expiries in the fixture (5-day and 30-day tenors), the far one is already the closest match; a chain with more expiries would need an actual tenor interpolation to produce a true constant-maturity 30-day IV, which this day doesn't build.
- **This is not yet the v0.6 contract.** The contract's `skew_25d` (a delta-selected strike, which needs Day 3's Greeks wired into the surface pipeline - not done here) and `iv_rank_1y` (a percentile over a real trailing year, which needs real trading days of history to mean anything - see the point above) are both left out rather than faked from two data points. This day only builds the history those two fields will eventually read.
- **The workflow's live-capture step is best-effort by design, per `volsurface.snapshot`'s own documented contract**: a failed live fetch exits non-zero and leaves the fixture exactly as it was, so `continue-on-error: true` is safe - a failure there means "still on the fixture," never "wrote something wrong." Untriggered so far: this workflow has not yet run on GitHub's infrastructure (only exercised locally via the CLI it calls), so whether NSE actually opens up to a GitHub-hosted runner is unverified.

Day 7 (25-delta skew, `volsurface/skew.py`, wired into `volsurface/history.py`; movement query, `volsurface/event_skew.py`): closes the gap Day 6 explicitly left open (`skew_25d` "not computed anywhere yet" - README Limitations) by finding the strike where a call's/put's delta equals ±0.25 within the *same* fitted expiry `atm_iv_30d` already reads from, then reading that strike's own fitted IV. A second CLI compares mean `skew_25d` in a window before vs. after a `--event-date`, using `data_date` (never `run_date`) to place rows in time - the same distinction Day 6 built the history file on.

- **Finding the 25-delta strike needs the strike and its own fitted IV at the same time, so it's a 1D root find, not a lookup.** Delta depends on sigma, and sigma here comes from the fitted smile evaluated *at* that same strike - `find_delta_strike` solves `delta(K, smile(K)) - target = 0` with Brent's method, bounded to the expiry's own fitted domain (the strikes Day 4 already filtered and arbitrage-checked), and raises `SkewError` rather than extrapolating if the target isn't bracketed there. On the committed RELIANCE fixture's far (30d-ish) expiry, both 25-delta strikes land comfortably inside the fitted domain (put near K=1340, call near K=1478, against a filtered range of [1275, 1525]); the near (5d) expiry's domain is narrower and was not checked to bracket ±0.25 for every possible chain.
- **The recovered skew is positive on this fixture, matching its own documented downward equity skew** (`skew_25d = iv(25-delta put) - iv(25-delta call)` ≈ +0.011 on the far expiry, `r=6.5%`, `q=0%`) - consistent with, not independent confirmation of, the fact that this chain was hand-assembled with IV rising as strike falls (Day 1 Findings). It shows the delta-selection machinery reproduces the skew shape that was already built into the smile, not that the machinery has been checked against a real, independently-observed skew.
- **`event_skew_report`'s actual answer today is "no comparison possible," and that's the honest state of this sandbox, not a bug.** Every row in `fixtures/history/RELIANCE.json` shares one `data_date` (Day 6's own finding: the fixture provider never advances it), so a pre-event/post-event split over real history rows is empty on both sides for any event date. The comparison logic itself - correct splitting by `data_date`, mean-across-a-window, explicit exclusion of rows with no `skew_25d` - is proven instead against synthetic multi-day history in `tests/test_event_skew.py` and by hand against a synthetic four-row file (pre-event mean 0.025, post-event mean 0.08, change +0.055), the same "prove the plumbing against synthetic data, be honest that the real fixture can't exercise it yet" pattern Day 4's arbitrage checks and Day 6's own history job both already used.
- **No actual scheduled-event calendar (earnings dates, policy announcements) is wired in.** `--event-date` is a plain user-supplied date; there is no lookup from an underlying to its own earnings/policy dates here or in any other repo in this portfolio, so today's "event" is whatever date the caller names, not a fetched one.
- 235/235 tests pass (217 prior + 18 new: 9 in `test_skew.py`, 9 in `test_event_skew.py`), CLI run by hand both against the real (single-row) fixture history and against a synthetic multi-day file to exercise the actual before/after comparison.

Day 8 (`ml-pipeline-audit`, `volsurface/audit.py`): a CLI that re-verifies the three "Done when" claims from `NEXT_STEPS.md` mechanically against the real fixture chain, then closes a real gap Day 4 and 5's own Findings named explicitly rather than papering over.

- **The real gap: "no arbitrage violations found" on the real fixture had never been distinguished from "the checks wouldn't fire even if there were one."** Every prior test proving `check_butterfly`/`check_calendar` actually catch something (`tests/test_surface.py`) built its bad smile entirely from scratch - three to five hand-picked strikes, never this project's own RELIANCE fixture. So Day 4's "no violations found" was consistent with either a genuinely clean surface or a check that silently never fires on this chain's specific shape (strike spacing, tenor, tick-rounded wings) - there was no way to tell which from the evidence on hand.
- **The audit closes that gap with two negative controls run against the real fixture's own strikes, not synthetic ones.** `audit_arbitrage_checks_fire_on_real_fixture` takes the real fixture's filtered, OTM-selected strikes for its near expiry, spikes one interior strike's real solved IV 5x, and requires `check_butterfly` to flag it - it does, as 49 violations across the fine repricing grid (one bad IV bends the re-priced call curve non-convex at many nearby grid points, not just the one strike it sits at). `audit_calendar_check_fires_on_real_fixture` shrinks the real far-expiry IVs to 5% of their fitted value and requires `check_calendar` to flag the resulting total-variance inversion against the real near expiry - it does, as 5 violations. Both checks are now proven to fire on data shaped like this exact chain, not just on hand-built cases.
- **This narrows the gap, it doesn't close it.** The negative controls still corrupt this one committed fixture, not an independent real chain with a genuine violation nobody planted - "the checks would catch a violation in a chain shaped like RELIANCE's" is a stronger claim than Day 4 could make, but "the checks would catch a violation in a real, different, noisy NSE chain" is still unverified. See Limitations.
- **The round-trip and Greeks cross-check audits found nothing new** - both were already exercised per-parameter-combination in `tests/test_iv.py` and `tests/test_greeks.py` - but now run once, end to end, against every row the real fixture actually produces: 50/50 solved contracts round-trip to worst error 3.77e-13 (rupees), and worst analytic-vs-finite-difference Greek gap is 8.44e-06, both far inside the audit's own tolerances (`ROUND_TRIP_TOL=1e-4`, `GREEKS_FD_TOL=1e-4`).
- **The audit functions are themselves tested against failure, not just success**: each has a test that monkeypatches the underlying check to always report clean and confirms the audit raises `AuditError` rather than reporting a false pass - an audit that can't fail is not actually checking anything.

## Limitations and what would make me wrong

- **`fixtures/chains/RELIANCE.json` is not a live capture.** Both live sources are blocked in practice from this sandbox (see Findings) - NSE returns HTTP 200 with an empty body, yfinance lists zero expiries for NSE single names. The fixture was built by assembling a plausible NSE-shaped payload (spot 1400.5, two expiries, 13 strikes, a downward equity skew, `r = 6.5%`, no dividend adjustment) and pricing every leg with Black-Scholes, then running that payload through the real `parse_nse_payload()` so the fixture is produced by the same code a live capture would use. It exercises the parser and the schema honestly; it does not exercise a real NSE response, and every IV computed against it in later days will be internally consistent by construction, not evidence the solver handles real market noise.
- Quotes go stale and spreads widen. A surface built from a thin snapshot describes the spread, not the volatility.
- Dividend and rate assumptions move implied vol; both are stated explicitly rather than buried.
- Strikes surviving the liquidity filter are a biased sample of the chain, skewed toward the money.
- `NSEChainProvider` and `YFinanceChainProvider` are implemented and unit-tested against injected payloads/mocks (`TestNSEChainProviderOffline`, `TestYFinanceChainProviderOffline`), but neither has been proven against a real successful response - only against the real *failure* modes this sandbox produced.
- **The IV solver has only ever seen a self-consistent synthetic chain.** Every price it has solved was itself produced by the same Black-Scholes formula it inverts, with no bid/ask noise beyond the fixture's own tick-rounding. A real NSE chain has crossed quotes, stale prints, and dividend/borrow effects this solver assumes away (`q=0` throughout - see `DEFAULT_DIVIDEND_YIELD` in `volsurface/iv.py`); none of that has been exercised yet.
- **The solver refuses two real, if narrow, classes of quote rather than guessing at them**: prices within `1e-6` of their own no-arbitrage floor (genuinely unrecoverable in double precision - see Findings) and prices that violate their no-arbitrage bound outright (as the two cent-rounded deepest-ITM legs in the RELIANCE fixture do). Both are correct refusals, not bugs, but they mean "IV solved for every contract" is not yet true for any chain, including the one committed here.
- `r=6.5%` and `q=0%` are CLI defaults (`--rate`/`--dividend-yield`), not calibrated to any real term structure; the recovered smile is only as trustworthy as that assumption.
- **Greeks are only as good as the IV they're computed at**, so they inherit every limitation above: `q=0` throughout, unproven against a real bid/ask spread, and unavailable for the two contracts the solver refuses. The finite-difference cross-check confirms the *formulas* are transcribed correctly, not that the Greeks would still line up this cleanly against noisy real quotes or a nonzero dividend yield - both untested here.
- **The Day 4 liquidity filter's thresholds (open interest >= 1000, relative spread <= 20%) are documented defaults, not calibrated** to any real NSE execution cost or this name's actual liquidity profile - see Findings for why open interest alone would have been the wrong signal on this fixture.
- **The Day 5 visualizations inherit every limitation above (fixture-only, self-consistent-by-construction, `q=0`, two thin expiries) and add two of their own.** First, plotting ATM IV against raw tenor (the term-structure chart) can look like a calendar-arbitrage violation on a chart even when the total-variance check that actually governs arbitrage passes cleanly - this fixture's own near-vs-far IV inversion is exactly that case, and the chart's axis label is the only thing preventing a reader from conflating the two; a chart of total variance instead would avoid the ambiguity entirely but was not built, since the term-structure slice's job here is showing *IV level*, which is what a trader reads off it, not restating Day 4's arbitrage check in picture form. Second, the "3D surface" never interpolates across expiries (see Day 5 Findings) - it is only ever as connected as the number of expiries in the underlying chain, and with two expiries here it is really two 3D lines, not a surface a reader could genuinely eyeball for a smooth or broken shape.
- **The butterfly/calendar arbitrage checks have only ever run *unmodified* against a chain that is arbitrage-free by construction** (this fixture was priced from one self-consistent Black-Scholes surface, then tick-rounded - see Findings), so "no violations found" on it still demonstrates the checks run end to end, not that a real violation would survive undetected in production use.
